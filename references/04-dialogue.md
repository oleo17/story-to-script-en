# 04 — Dialogue

English dialogue has one job that non-dialogue never has: it must sound like it was overheard rather than written.

Most AI-generated dialogue fails in a specific, recognizable way. It is grammatically complete, emotionally explicit, evenly distributed among characters, and answers every question it raises. Real speech is none of those things.

---

## 1. The three failures

### 1.1 On-the-nose

The character says what they feel or want.

> **BAD** — "I'm furious that you didn't tell me about the money."
>
> **BETTER** — "How much did you take out?"
> "Does it matter?"
> "It matters that you thought I wouldn't notice."

The feeling is never named. It is inferred from what they choose to talk about.

### 1.2 Complete sentences

Spoken English is fragmentary. People answer different questions than the ones asked, start sentences they abandon, and use the other person's words back at them.

> **BAD** — "I don't believe that you actually forgot our anniversary."
>
> **BETTER** — "You forgot."
> "I didn't forget. I was—"
> "You forgot."

Repetition of two words does more than a full sentence of accusation.

### 1.3 Even distribution

Every character speaks in the same length, rhythm, and register, because one writer wrote them. Real ensembles are lopsided: one person talks too much, one answers in single words, one interrupts.

## 2. Subtext

Subtext is not "hidden meaning." It is **two people talking about different things in the same conversation.**

The most reliable construction:

```text
A wants to talk about X.
B refuses to talk about X, so B answers every question as though it were about Y.
The scene is about X, and every line is about Y.
```

### 2.1 Worked example

Situation: he's leaving tomorrow. Neither will say it.

> **HER** — "You packed."
> **HIM** — "It's a small bag."
> **HER** — "It's a small bag for two weeks."
> **HIM** — "It's ten days."
> **HER** — "Ten."
> **HIM** — "I'll be back before the lease is up."
> **HER** — "I'm not worried about the lease."

Nothing about the relationship is stated. The scene is entirely about whether he's coming back, and it's conducted over luggage and paperwork.

### 2.2 Subtext failure modes

| Failure | Looks like | Why it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| **No second layer** | Lines mean exactly what they say | Nothing to play; actor has only one note |
| **Both layers stated** | Character says the subtext out loud "for clarity" | Destroys the subtext entirely |
| **Random second layer** | Lines appear to mean something, but it's unresolvable | Feels vague, not deep |
| **Subtext with no surface** | Dialogue is pure implication, no concrete topic | Audience loses the thread |

The surface topic must be solid enough to stand on its own. "We're fighting about the dishes" is a surface. "We're fighting about us" is not a scene.

## 3. Voice fingerprints

Each speaking character needs a fingerprint that survives being covered with a hand over the name.

### 3.1 The five dials

Set each one for each lead. Differences of one notch are audible; differences of three are unmistakable.

| Dial | Low end | High end |
|---|---|---|
| **Verbosity** | Answers in fragments | Speaks in paragraphs |
| **Register** | Plain, monosyllabic | Formal, Latinate |
| **Directness** | Asks, deflects, implies | States, demands, declares |
| **Self-disclosure** | Volunteers nothing | Volunteers everything |
| **Interruption** | Waits, yields | Talks over, finishes others' sentences |

### 3.2 Fingerprint card

```text
@voice: DANA
Verbosity:       low — rarely more than 12 words
Register:        plain; drops articles when angry ("Wrong floor.")
Directness:      high, but about facts, never feelings
Self-disclosure: none
Interruption:    doesn't interrupt — goes quiet instead
Never says:      "I feel", "I need", "please"
Signature move:  answers a question with a correction
```

**"Never says"** is the highest-value field. A character with a prohibited word becomes recognizable fast, and the one moment they break the prohibition is free drama.

### 3.3 Register as class and background

In English, register is the fastest way to indicate class, education, and region without exposition.

| Marker | Reads as |
|---|---|
| Contractions throughout | Casual, native, most natural speech |
| No contractions at all | Formal, foreign, or guarded — *choose deliberately* |
| Latinate vocabulary ("commence," "regarding") | Educated, institutional, or putting on airs |
| Germanic/short words ("start," "about") | Plain, direct, working |
| Sentence-final "though," "like," "mind" | Regional, informal |
| Dropping the subject ("Can't do it.") | Familiarity with the listener |

Contractions are the default. A character who never contracts is making a statement — make sure the script knows what statement.

## 4. Rhythm and the read-aloud test

### 4.1 Read it out loud

Not metaphorically. Read the line at 2.5 words per second and see if it can be said without a breath in a place that ruins it.

| Problem | Symptom when read aloud |
|---|---|
| Too many subordinate clauses | Runs out of air mid-sentence |
| Stacked adjectives | Sounds like ad copy |
| Same stress pattern every line | Sounds like a metronome |
| Tongue-twisters | Physical stumble, actor will change it |

### 4.2 Vary the line length

Three consecutive long lines flatten a scene. Three consecutive short lines become a shouting match. The pattern that works: long, long, short. The short line lands.

> "I sat in that parking lot for two hours because I thought if I went in first I'd be the one who apologized." *(long)*
> "You always do that. You decide what happened and then you live in it." *(long)*
> "I waited." *(short — lands)*

### 4.3 The pause

`(beat)` in parentheses is usually a sign the line is doing too little. A better fix: cut the line before it and let the silence be the reaction.

Use a pause in dialogue only when:
- Something new has just been learned by a character
- A lie has just been told
- A decision is being made on screen

Otherwise the pause is a gap, and gaps read as weakness.

## 5. Short-form dialogue rules

Vertical drama and social narrative have constraints that film dialogue doesn't.

### 5.1 Everything must survive mute

The dramatic information in a line must also be visible. If the reveal only exists in audio, half the first watches miss it.

- Put the object on screen when it's named
- Let a character's face register before they speak the line
- Burn in captions; assume the platform will

### 5.2 Front-load the operative word

Phone screens truncate attention in the first three words.

> **WEAK** — "I think you should probably know that your sister has been lying to you."
>
> **STRONG** — "Your sister's been lying."

### 5.3 One idea per line, and no line longer than 3 seconds

A 3-second line at 2.5 wps is 7–8 words. Anything longer is two lines, or a monologue the format can't afford.

### 5.4 The confrontation engine

Vertical drama is built on two-person confrontation in close quarters. The rhythm that works:

```text
accusation → denial → evidence → escalation → reversal
```

Four to six exchanges. Each exchange shorter than the last, until the reversal, which is one line.

> "You knew."
> "I didn't."
> "You were in the room."
> "I was in the room."
> "Then say it."
> "..."
> "Say it."
> "I signed the papers."

## 6. Dialogue word budget

Recall from `00-scale-control.md`: dialogue runs **2.5 words/sec**, ceiling **3.0**.

| Runtime | Max dialogue words | Typical line count |
|---|---|---|
| 15 s | 38 | 4–6 |
| 30 s | 75 | 8–12 |
| 60 s | 150 | 16–24 |
| 90 s | 225 | 24–36 |
| 2 min | 300 | 32–48 |

If a draft exceeds these, the fix order is:

1. Delete redundant lines (usually 10–20% of any first draft)
2. Convert a line to a look
3. Merge two characters into one speaker
4. Cut a whole exchange

Never fix an overrun by making the delivery faster.

## 7. Specific English pitfalls

### 7.1 "As you know, Bob"

Exposition between two people who already know the facts, delivered for the audience.

> **BAD** — "As you know, the company's been in your mother's family since 1962, and after the merger it went to your uncle."
>
> **BETTER** — "Sixty-two years in this family. And it goes to *him*."

The second delivers the same facts as an accusation, which is a reason to say them out loud.

### 7.2 Over-explaining the emotional stakes

> **BAD** — "If you walk out that door, I will never forgive you, and you'll lose the only person who ever believed in you."
>
> **BETTER** — "The door's not locked."

### 7.3 Adverb-loaded dialogue tags

Tags are for clarity, not performance. `she said, angrily` is a note to the actor that the line failed to deliver.

In scripts, dialogue tags usually disappear entirely — the character cue already says who's speaking. Save tags for prose adaptations.

### 7.4 Naming the relationship

> **BAD** — "You're my ex-husband. You don't get to ask me that."

The audience knows. Naming the relationship tells them nothing and costs a line's worth of time.

**Exception:** in vertical drama, the relationship label is often the hook, and stating it in the first 3 seconds is deliberate. Use it as a hook, never as a reminder.

### 7.5 Regional and period consistency

If a character is British, they don't say "gotten." If the story is 1970s, nobody says "reach out." One inconsistency breaks the illusion for anyone who notices — and those are the people who review.

### 7.6 Profanity

Profanity is not intensity. It is a register choice. A character who swears in every line has used up the register; a character who swears once in ninety seconds owns the room. Decide the policy per character, then obey it.

## 8. Dialogue pass output format

```text
[DIALOGUE PASS]
Script: <title>   Lines: n   Dialogue words: n / ceiling n

DIAGNOSIS
  On-the-nose:            n instances (worst: <line ref>)
  Exposition delivery:    n
  Repeated information:   n
  Interchangeable voices: yes/no (which characters)
  Over-length lines:      n (> 8 words for short-form)

REWRITES (line-by-line, worst first)
  <scene/moment>
    OLD: "<line>"
    NEW: "<line>"
    Why: <one clause>

VOICE CARDS (updated, if fingerprints were missing)
  @voice: <NAME> ...

BUDGET
  dialogue words: n → n after rewrites
```
