# 02 — Drafting the Script

Writing the actual pages. This module assumes a locked Spec Card and a project bible. If those don't exist, go back to `00` and `01` first — drafting without a budget is how you end up with a 4-minute "60-second" piece.

---

## 1. Two formats, and when to use which

### 1.1 Standard screenplay format (production / cinematic)

Use for shorts, films, anything that will be shot by a crew or read by a director.

```text
INT. APARTMENT HALLWAY - NIGHT

Rain hammers the window at the end of the corridor. Fluorescent
light stutters.

MARCUS (34), soaked, holds a cardboard box against his chest.
He does not knock.

He reads the name on the door again. It hasn't changed.

He knocks.

Beat. The chain slides. The door opens exactly four inches.

DANA (32) looks at the box, not at him.

                    DANA
          Wrong floor.

                    MARCUS
          It's addressed to you.

She doesn't take it. The rain keeps going.

                                                       CUT TO:
```

**Rules that matter:**
- Scene headings in caps: `INT./EXT. LOCATION - DAY/NIGHT`. Use `CONTINUOUS` when the next scene follows immediately.
- Action lines in **present tense**, active voice, third person. Only what the camera can see or the mic can hear.
- Character cue in caps on its own line, first appearance gets an age in parentheses.
- Parentheticals sparingly — only when the line would be misread without them. `(beat)` is a crutch.
- Transitions only when they do work. `CUT TO:` at the end of every scene is noise; save it for hard cuts that matter.
- No camera directions in a spec script unless the shot is the point.

### 1.2 Vertical short-form script format

Use for ReelShort-style episodes, TikTok narrative, motion comics, and anything that will be produced shot-by-shot from the document.

```text
[SCENE 1] INT. HALLWAY - NIGHT - 0:00-0:08   (2.5 s/shot)

SHOT 1.1  CU. Door chain sliding. | SFX: chain, rain
SHOT 1.2  MCU. Dana's face, four inches of doorway. | no line
SHOT 1.3  OTS. Marcus holding the box, rain in hair. |
          MARCUS: "It's addressed to you."
SHOT 1.4  CU. The label. Her name, his handwriting underneath.

HOOK: 0:02 — she says "Wrong floor" while looking at the box.
```

**Rules that matter here:**
- Every shot carries a **function**: hook / establish / action / reaction / reveal / transition / payoff.
- Shot size, subject, and audio in one line. If it doesn't fit in one line, it's two shots.
- Time codes per scene block, not per shot — per-shot timing is a storyboard job (`06`).
- The hook is marked explicitly. If you can't mark it, there isn't one.

## 2. The opening: first two seconds

For any format with a retention problem, the opening is not story. It is the price of admission.

### 2.1 Four hook types that work

| Type | Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **In extremis** | Start at the worst moment | A woman on her knees in a wedding dress, blood on the hem |
| **Contradiction** | Two things that cannot both be true | A man in a tuxedo eating from a dumpster |
| **Question with stakes** | A visible anomaly that demands explanation | Every clock in the room reads 3:07 |
| **Interrupted action** | Mid-gesture, no context | A hand pulls back from a doorbell, then the door opens anyway |

### 2.2 Hooks that don't work

- A logo, title card, or fade-in
- An establishing shot of a city
- Someone waking up
- Narration explaining the premise
- A slow push toward a door that will open at 0:12
- Any line that starts with "Let me tell you about..."

### 2.3 The hook test

Read only the first 5 seconds aloud. If a stranger cannot state (a) who is in trouble, (b) roughly what kind of story this is, and (c) one reason to keep watching — rebuild it.

## 3. Scene construction

### 3.1 Every scene needs an unanswered question at its end

Not a cliffhanger — just an open loop. If a scene resolves cleanly, the audience has permission to leave.

| Loop type | Example |
|---|---|
| Information withheld | He pockets the letter without reading it |
| Decision deferred | She picks up the phone, doesn't dial |
| Contradiction planted | He says he's never been here; his coat is on the hook |
| Threat introduced | A car pulls up outside and stays |

### 3.2 The three-line scene

Strip every scene to three lines and check whether it still functions:

```text
Who wants what in this scene?
Who or what blocks it?
What changed by the end?
```

If line three is "nothing," the scene is a transition wearing a costume.

### 3.3 Enter late, leave early

Start the scene after the setup, end it before the resolution. Vertically-formatted drama especially: a phone screen has no patience for a handshake and a sit-down.

**Cut list, applied to every scene before delivery:**
- Greetings, goodbyes, "hello," "thank you," "I'll be going now"
- Walking through doors and down corridors, unless the corridor is the point
- Any line that restates the previous line
- Any reaction shot that repeats the reaction already given

## 4. Action lines

Action lines are the most under-written part of most AI-assisted scripts. They are not stage directions. They are the film.

### 4.1 Rules

1. **Present tense, active voice.** "He opens the box." Not "He is opening the box." Not "The box is opened by him."
2. **Only filmable information.** Interior states are invisible unless they produce behavior. "She is devastated" → "She keeps folding the same shirt."
3. **One idea per paragraph.** Blocks of four lines or more become unreadable and unfilmable.
4. **Sound is action.** If it's audible, it belongs here: `A phone buzzes inside the wall.`
5. **Caps for first appearances and sounds.** `MARCUS (34)` on first appearance; `BANG` for a sound cue that matters.
6. **No similes for the camera's benefit.** "The light falls like forgiveness" is a note to yourself, not a shot.

### 4.2 The specificity test

Weak:

> She looks sad and walks away.

Strong:

> She starts to speak. Doesn't. Puts the ring on the counter, face down.

The second is one shot. The first is a request for the director to invent something.

### 4.3 What to cut from action lines

- Weather that does nothing
- Room description beyond what a character interacts with
- Wardrobe detail that never recurs
- Adverbs attached to verbs: "quickly," "suddenly," "angrily"
- Anything the audience cannot perceive in one shot

## 5. Pacing mechanics

### 5.1 Runtime control levers

When a draft is over budget, these are the levers in order of least damage:

| Lever | Saves | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Cut redundant dialogue | 5–15 s | Nothing, if truly redundant |
| Convert a line to a look | 3–8 s | Some clarity |
| Merge two scenes into one location | 10–20 s | A transition beat |
| Cut a reaction shot | 1–2 s | Emotional registration |
| Cut a whole beat | 20–30 s | A grain — the most expensive cut |
| Speed up delivery | 3–5 s | Performance quality — **avoid** |

### 5.2 Pacing by section

Do not hold one density for a whole piece. A workable default for a 60–90 s vertical episode:

```text
0:00–0:05   hook         2.0–2.5 s/shot
0:05–0:20   setup        3.5 s/shot
0:20–0:50   escalation   3.0 s/shot
0:50–1:10   turn         4.0 s/shot   (slowing down is a signal)
1:10–1:20   cliff        2.0 s/shot
```

The turn gets slower because the audience needs a beat to absorb it. Speeding up through a reversal is the most common pacing error in short-form.

### 5.3 Silence is a beat, not a gap

A held shot with no dialogue buys more tension than a line of dialogue ever can — but only where the audience already knows what's at stake. Silence before the audience knows anything is just a pause.

## 6. Drafting order

Write out of order. The physical order of writing has nothing to do with the order of the film.

1. **The turn** — the reversal or reveal that the whole piece exists for. Write it first, at full strength.
2. **The hook** — the first 2 seconds that make the turn watchable.
3. **The bridge** — the minimum material that gets from hook to turn without stalling.
4. **The exit** — the last image. Decide whether it resolves, loops, or cliffs.
5. **The connective tissue** — everything else, written last, at minimum volume.

Writing front-to-back produces scripts that start strong and lose direction, because the strongest material got spent on setup.

## 7. Self-check before delivering a draft

```text
[DRAFT CHECK]
Runtime:              X s  (budget Y)
Script words:         n    (budget n)
Dialogue words:       n    (ceiling n)
Shots:                n    (budget n)
Hook:                 present at 0:0X / missing
Turn:                 at 0:XX / missing
Ending:               resolve / loop / cliff
Grain count:          n    (matches spine? y/n)
Characters speaking:  n    (over cast cap? y/n)
Locations:            n
Lines that could be cut: n   (if > 10% of lines, cut them now)
```

Deliver the draft **and** this check. A draft without its budget audit is a guess.

---

## 8. Formatting conventions that reviewers notice

Small things that make a script read as professional versus generated:

- **Don't** use markdown headings inside a screenplay. Scene headings are the headings.
- **Don't** bold character names. Caps is the convention.
- **Do** keep action lines under four lines.
- **Do** leave a blank line between action blocks and dialogue.
- **Do** number scenes only if the script will be shot from the document (short-form format). Numbered scenes in a spec screenplay look amateur.
- **Do** keep the slugline locations consistent — `INT. KITCHEN` and `INT. THE KITCHEN` are two locations to a continuity supervisor and a scheduling headache to a producer.
